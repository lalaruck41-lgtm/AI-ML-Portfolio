Machine Learning vs. Deep Learning in Customer Analytics

How should an analytics team match a modeling approach to the customer problem and the data available? This individual project compares a traditional machine-learning approach for structured churn data with transformer-based natural language processing for unstructured customer feedback.

Individual academic projectSupport Vector MachinesTransformer-based NLPCustomer analytics
The business question

Different customer questions require different data approaches

Customer churn prediction typically draws on structured records such as service usage, contract details, and customer characteristics. Understanding customer sentiment, by contrast, often requires interpreting unstructured text such as reviews and open-ended survey responses.

The project examines why a traditional machine-learning model and a deep-learning language model may be appropriate for these distinct tasks, and what each approach contributes to business understanding.

Methods explored

Two approaches, two data types

01 · Structured data

Support Vector Machine (SVM)

Explored in the context of churn prediction using customer-level features, such as service usage, contract information, and customer characteristics.

Business use: identify patterns associated with customers who may be at risk of leaving.

02 · Unstructured text

Transformer-based NLP

Explored for interpreting customer reviews and open-ended survey responses, where context and relationships between words matter.

Business use: organize feedback, surface recurring themes, and understand customer sentiment at scale.

Scope note: this is a comparative academic exploration of the techniques and their applications. It is not presented as a production model deployment or a benchmark experiment.

Analytical considerations

Choosing a method thoughtfully

  • Data structure: determine whether the input is tabular customer data or natural-language text.
  • Problem definition: define the business outcome before selecting a model.
  • Interpretability: consider how stakeholders will understand and act on model outputs.
  • Evaluation: use measures aligned with the task, and consider the consequences of false positives and false negatives.
  • Operational fit: account for data availability, computational requirements, and ongoing maintenance.
Key takeaway

Model choice should follow the business problem — not the other way around.

Traditional machine learning and deep learning are complementary tools. Their value depends on the data, the decision to be supported, and the practical requirements of the organization.
